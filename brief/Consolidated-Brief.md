## 12. How Many Towns Are Really Out There? (The Census Correction)

The thread's estimate is 10,000 to 20,000 towns with 5,000 to 40,000 people. The 20,000 figure appears to be the count of **all** incorporated places in America, of every size:

| Funnel step | Count | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| All incorporated places in the US | **~19,500** | US Census Bureau |
| With fewer than 5,000 people | ~14,800 (76%) | US Census Bureau |
| With 50,000 or more people | ~780 (4.0%) | US Census Bureau |
| **In the target band: 5,000–40,000 people** | **~3,700** | Calculated from the Census figures above |
| Also 1+ hour from a major city (estimate) | **~900–1,400** | Modeled — see note |
| Also passing the need screen (no taxi, no reliable rideshare, DUI problem) | **~550–850** | Modeled — see note |

**Why the distance estimate is ~900–1,400:** the Census Bureau counts **542 micropolitan areas** — population centers of 10,000–50,000 that sit apart from metropolitan areas, often 100+ miles from a major city. Their anchor towns are the core of this market. Added to them are in-band towns in non-metro counties outside any metro/micropolitan area (America has 1,958 non-metro counties holding 46 million people). Towns inside a metro's orbit mostly fail the project's own 1-hour test because they sit within commuting range of a major city, which is also why they still have Uber drivers.

**What this means for the money:** the honest ceiling is smaller than the thread's, and the revenue math in Appendix B has been rebuilt on these counts. The counterweight: counties are a second buyer the thread does not count (a county can buy for its unincorporated areas and small towns), and nothing in the model caps a town at $1,000/month forever. But any pitch that leads with "20,000 towns × $1,000" will fail the first diligence check. Lead with the funnel.

**Supporting evidence that the need is real in these towns:** a South Carolina review of state TNC (Uber/Lyft) fee data found **103 of 271 incorporated towns and cities (38%) had zero rideshare trips originate in them over 2.5 years**, and officials in those towns reported no private ride options at all. Rural rideshare fails on economics: long distances, low density, and drivers who can earn more by leaving for the metro — exactly the Grass Valley pattern in the thread.

## 13. What a DUI Actually Costs a Town (Verifying the $1,500 Claim)

The thread's pitch figure is ~$1,500 taxpayer cost per DUI. Two independent sources say that figure is, if anything, conservative:

- **California municipal courts study (US Office of Justice Programs / National Institute of Justice):** tracked 152 DUI cases through every agency that touched them — arrest, chemical testing, jail, court, District Attorney, Public Defender, probation, DMV actions. **Mean government processing cost: $1,108 per case** (median $649) in the study's dollars. Adjusted for inflation, that mean is **~$2,700 in 2026 dollars**.
- **Washington State Criminal Justice Cost Model (2023, used in official legislative fiscal notes):** combined law enforcement, prosecution, and public defense cost to process one alcohol-offense incident: **~$6,470**, plus jail at **~$145 per day**.

**Officer time:** Chief Dan Foss's 2–6 hours (thread) sits inside the independent range. A Michigan police chief justifying a DUI cost-recovery ordinance stated a drunk-driving arrest takes **at least 2 hours** and "more paperwork and forms than just about anything else"; processing accounts from other departments run **5–7 hours**. At a loaded officer cost of ~$60/hour, officer time alone is roughly **$120–$450 per DUI**.

**How many DUIs a target town sees:** the FBI recorded **1,031,812 DUI arrests in the US in 2021** — about **3.1 arrests per 1,000 residents per year**, with non-metro arrest volumes comparable to or higher than metro. Applied to the target band:

| Town size | Est. DUI arrests / year | Public processing burden @ $1,500 (thread figure) | @ ~$2,700 (CA study, inflation-adj.) | @ $6,470 (WA 2023 model) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6,000 (Nevada City) | ~19 | $28,500 / yr | ~$51,700 / yr | ~$122,900 / yr |
| 12,000 (Grass Valley) | ~37 | $55,500 / yr | ~$100,700 / yr | ~$239,400 / yr |
| 20,000 | ~62 | $93,000 / yr | ~$168,700 / yr | ~$401,100 / yr |
| 40,000 | ~124 | $186,000 / yr | ~$337,400 / yr | ~$802,300 / yr |

Against a **$12,000/year** fee, even the smallest target town on the stingiest figure is spending more than twice the fee processing DUIs — before counting a single crash. Two caveats an investor will raise, stated up front: (1) processing costs are split across city, county, and state budgets, so the pitch is to *taxpayers*, not to one city ledger; (2) small towns that serve as their county's nightlife hub will run above these per-capita estimates, not below.

**The crash side is bigger than the processing side:** alcohol-impaired crashes cost the US an estimated **$44–$59 billion per year** in economic terms (NHTSA/CDC range), and **$123 billion+** when quality-of-life losses are included. **12,429 people died in alcohol-impaired crashes in 2023**, roughly a third of all US traffic deaths. The burden is rural: **43–52% of alcohol-impaired driving deaths occur in rural areas, where only ~19–20% of the population lives** (NHTSA data via the National Academies) — a per-capita burden roughly 2.5× the urban rate. The average alcohol-related crash costs a community about **$56,000** (Wisconsin program data, Section 14).

## 14. Proof This Model Works (Comparable Programs)

- **Wisconsin "Road Crew" (University of Wisconsin / NHTSA-supported pilot):** a rural ride-home program in 3 communities gave **19,757 rides in one year** (~6,600 per community), estimated to have **prevented 15 alcohol-related crashes — a 17% reduction**. Cost per avoided crash: **$15,300**, against a ~$56,000 average cost of an alcohol-related crash — roughly a **3.7× return**.
- **Tavern League of Wisconsin SafeRide:** running since 1985; **45,527 free rides in 2022–23** from 1,730 establishments; **over 1,000,000 rides since 2004**; program cost **~$1 million per year statewide** (≈ $11–$22 per ride across program years), funded by bars plus a surcharge on OWI convictions. Wisconsin OWI convictions are down **44% since 2007**, with SafeRide credited as a contributor.
- **What this implies for Local Drivers Network:** proven programs deliver rides at roughly **$11–$22 per ride** all-in. A town paying $12,000/year breaks even against those benchmarks at only **~550–1,100 rides per year** — a few rides a night. The model is not experimental; what is new is packaging it as software a town buys instead of a program a state or tavern league runs.

## 17. How a Town Pays (Funding Channels)

The $12,000/year does not have to come out of a small town's general fund. Documented channels:

1. **Federal highway safety money, flowing through the state.** NHTSA grant programs approved **$800M+ for FY2026** (Section 402: $409M; Section 405: $382M), including **$147.5M for impaired-driving countermeasures** in a recent cycle. States pass this money to local projects through their highway safety offices; alternative-transportation / safe-ride programs are a recognized countermeasure category. Safe Streets and Roads for All (SS4A) is a second federal safety channel.
2. **Offender-funded cost recovery (the Wisconsin and Michigan models).** Wisconsin funds SafeRide partly with a **surcharge on every OWI conviction**. Michigan cities use **cost-recovery ordinances** to bill DUI offenders for arrest costs. The test market already does this: **Grass Valley charges $2.00 per minute for DUI investigation and response** — at an average 3-hour DUI (Section 13), that is ~$360 per DUI, and at ~37 DUIs/year it recovers **~$13,300/year — enough to cover the entire $12,000 fee from offender payments alone.**
3. **Local co-funding, as proven by SafeRide:** bars and taverns co-fund rides in Wisconsin because fewer DUIs means their patrons get home and come back. The same split (town + bars + county health / hospital community-benefit money) is available town by town.

## Appendix A — Research: Typical Records Request Fees in Small Cities (Under 20,000 Population)

*This appendix answers the open task in the thread: "Go ahead and find out what a typical records request fee is in cities under a 20k population." Researched October 20, 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.*

### A.1 The California rule (most small towns in the test area are in California)

Under the **California Public Records Act (CPRA)**:

- **Inspecting records in person is free.**
- An agency may charge only the **direct cost of duplication** for copies. Courts and agency schedules put this at typically **$0.10 to $0.25 per page**.
- An agency generally **may not charge for staff time spent searching for, retrieving, or reviewing records**.
- Records that **already exist in electronic form** (PDF, spreadsheet, database export) are commonly provided by email / portal at **no charge or minimal charge**.
- **The exception that can get expensive:** if a request requires the agency to **compile data, run custom queries, or do programming** to produce a record that does not already exist, the requester can be charged the full cost of that production, including staff/programmer time. This is the charge Rene likely ran into warnings about in testing, and it can run into **hundreds of dollars per agency** if the request is framed as "go calculate this for us."
- Agencies must respond with a determination within **10 calendar days** (with a limited extension possible).

**Practical answer to the question asked:** for a normal, narrow request in a California city under 20,000 people, expect **$0 to about $25 per agency** — most often $0 if the record is delivered electronically, or a small flat record-request fee plus a per-page charge if paper copies are produced.

### A.2 What the two test towns actually charge

**Grass Valley, CA (~12,000) — City fee schedule:**
- Police **Record Request Fee: $5.00 for the first 10 pages, plus $0.25 per additional page**
- General document copies: **$0.10 per page**
- Note: Grass Valley also lists a separate **DUI Investigation and Reports charge of $2.00 per minute** of police response time. That is a cost-recovery charge billed in connection with a DUI incident itself, not a records-request fee — but it is directly relevant to the project's DUI cost model, because it shows the city already prices DUI response by the minute.

**Nevada County Sheriff (covers Nevada City, ~6,000):**
- **$5.00 for the first page and $0.50 for each page thereafter** for report copies.

Both are consistent with the pattern in the thread: the Grass Valley / Nevada City tests cost **$0**, because a short electronic or in-person request in a small agency often comes in under, or outside, the copy-fee schedule.

### A.3 Fee categories — why no special status is needed in California

- Under the **federal FOIA** (federal agencies only), requesters are put into fee categories, and news-media requesters pay reduced fees. That category does not apply to city police, sheriffs, or the Highway Patrol.
- City and county records in California are governed by the **CPRA**, which has **no special requester category and no purpose test** — anyone may request public records, and the fee limit (duplication cost only) is the same for everyone.
- So in California, the same low fee applies to every requester, with no status to claim and no purpose to state. In other states, fee and waiver rules vary by state public-records law — some states do have media or public-interest fee waivers, and some allow search fees. This needs a state-by-state check before Version 2.0 scales beyond California.

### A.4 What this means for a 20,000-town rollout — budget scenarios

The per-request fee is small. The risk is volume multiplied by requests that trigger the data-compilation exception.

| Scenario per town (assume 1–3 agencies per town) | Cost per town | Cost at 1,000 towns | Cost at 20,000 towns |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electronic delivery of an existing record | $0 | $0 | $0 |
| Typical small paper/electronic request | ~$5–$25 | ~$5,000–$25,000 | ~$100,000–$500,000 |
| Request requires data compilation / custom query | ~$100–$500+ | ~$100,000–$500,000+ | Not viable — must be avoided |

**Working rules for Version 2.0 that keep the rollout near $0:**

1. **Ask only for records that already exist** (e.g., DUI arrest counts / logs, calls-for-service data, existing reports and fee schedules) — never ask an agency to calculate a new statistic. The CPRA does not require an agency to create a record or answer a question; it requires it to produce existing records. Questions 4 and 5 in Section 5 therefore have to be converted into requests for existing source records, and the calculation done inside Rene's app.
2. **Request electronic format** and delivery by email wherever possible.
3. **Put a fee cap in every request** — e.g., "Please notify me and do not proceed if fees will exceed $25" — so no agency can generate a surprise bill.
4. **Pilot pricing first:** run the full request sequence in 10–20 towns, log every fee quoted, and only then project a national records budget.
5. **Track fees per agency type** (city police vs. sheriff vs. Highway Patrol) — the CHP and sheriffs run on different schedules than a small city PD.

### B.2 Build-cost break-even

- Version 1.0 cost to market: **$35,000**
- At $1,000/month per town, that is **35 town-months** — for example, **35 towns paying for one month, or 5 towns paying for seven months**, cover the quoted build cost (before the other path-to-revenue costs estimated in Section 18).

### B.3 The town's return, as pitched — and as verified

- Service cost: **$1,000 / month ($12,000 / year)**
- Thread figure: one DUI costs taxpayers **~$1,500** and **2–6 hours** of officer time
- Verified range (Section 13): **~$2,700–$6,470** in public processing cost per DUI in current dollars, and a typical 12,000-person town processes **~37 DUIs a year** — a public burden of roughly **$100,000–$240,000 a year**, against the $12,000 fee
- On any of these figures, the service pays for itself if it prevents **a handful of DUIs per year**, and the Wisconsin evidence (Section 14) shows ride-home programs preventing crashes at roughly a **3.7× return** on program cost

## 11. Open Questions and Risks

These are gaps or flags in the material as shared — none of them are answered in the screenshots:

1. **What exactly does the $35,000 cover?** No scope, feature list, timeline, or payment schedule for the LUSTRA LABS build was included.
2. **Terms not reviewed here.** The two-year noncompete and the terms of responsibility contract are mentioned, but their text was not in the screenshots.
3. **Records requests cannot compel an answer.** Public-records law requires agencies to produce existing records, not to answer questions or create new statistics. The DUI time/cost questions (Section 5) must be built from source records — see Appendix A.4.
4. **Fee exposure at scale.** Individually trivial fees become a real budget at 20,000 towns, and compilation-type requests can be billed at staff cost. Fee caps per request are essential.
5. **Requests are filed in the project's own name.** No special requester status is needed or claimed — in California the CPRA fee limit applies to every requester (Appendix A.3). Outside California, rules differ by state.
6. **Insurance figure is a negotiation target, not a signed rate.** The $0.30/mile figure depends on a future negotiation with HUM. The current figure in the thread is $0.60/mile.
7. **Transportation regulation varies.** Driver licensing, insurance minimums, and whether a town-run driver network is regulated as transportation-for-hire differ by state (and sometimes by city). The HUM model is described as not requiring commercial registration — that should be confirmed state by state before a town signs.
8. **Market size — now checked, and smaller than stated.** The 10,000–20,000 town range is the project's own estimate. The Census check in Part II, Section 12 puts in-band towns at ~3,700, distance-qualified towns at ~900–1,400, and need-qualified towns at ~550–850. Version 2.0's Step 1 search will produce the exact list; pitches should use the Section 12 funnel, not the 20,000 figure.
9. **Who pays for what is not fully specified.** The town pays $1,000/month and drivers keep 100% of fares and pay per-mile insurance. What the maintenance fee covers operationally (hosting, support, insurance administration, marketing) is not itemized in the messages.
10. **Decision pending in the thread.** The last open ask in the screenshots — the typical records request fee for cities under 20,000 population — is answered in Appendix A: typically **$0–$25**, most often **$0** for electronic delivery of an existing record in California.
