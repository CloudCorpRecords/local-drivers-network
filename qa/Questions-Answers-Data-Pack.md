## Q1. How many towns can actually buy this?

The honest funnel, from Census counts — not the round numbers that circulate in early pitches:

| Step | Towns |
|---|---|
| All incorporated places in the US | ~19,500 |
| With fewer than 5,000 people (excluded — too small to staff drivers) | −14,800 |
| With 50,000+ people (excluded — rideshare already serves them) | −780 |
| **In the 5,000–40,000 band** | **~3,700** |
| Also 1+ hour from a major city | ~900–1,400 |
| Also pass the need screen (taxi gone, rideshare absent, alcohol crashes a live problem) | **~550–850** |

At $12,000/yr per town, ~700 serviceable towns = **$8.4M ARR**, with a $44.4M ceiling. Not counted: county contracts covering unincorporated areas (counties, not towns, hold much rural road mileage), size-based price tiers, and insurance-administration revenue.

## Q2. What actually happened to rides in these towns?

- **The taxi era ended.** Small-town cab companies were thin-margin businesses; most closed over the past two decades and were not replaced.
- **Rideshare does not fill the gap.** Platform drivers cluster where trip density pays. In South Carolina, **103 of 271 incorporated towns and cities (38%) recorded zero Uber/Lyft trips originating over 2.5 years** of state TNC fee data; three entire counties recorded none. In the project's pilot market, drivers based in Grass Valley, CA migrated to Sacramento, an hour away, for better earnings.
- **The risk concentrates exactly there.** Rural areas produce **43–52% of US alcohol-impaired driving deaths** with about a fifth of the population, and the distances that make rides scarce also make crashes deadlier and emergency response slower.

## Q4. What does one DUI cost the public — really?

| Study | Figure | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| US Office of Justice Programs / NIJ — California municipal courts | $1,108 mean per case (study dollars) ≈ **~$2,700 in 2026 dollars** | Arrest through DMV action, labor only |
| Washington State Criminal Justice Cost Model (2023) | **~$6,470 per incident** | Law enforcement + prosecution + public defense; jail ~$145/day extra |
| Officer time per DUI | **2–6 hours** | Nevada City, CA police chief; corroborated by Michigan municipal reporting ("at least 2 hours," the most paperwork of any arrest) |

The project's own early pitch figure (~$1,500) **understates** both studies. The conservative number is the CA figure; either way, one town's existing DUI processing spend is a multiple of the service fee.

## Q5. Can a small town actually approve $1,000/month?

- **Price point:** $12,000/yr sits below most municipal competitive-bid/RFP thresholds for services, and below mainstream small-city software (Tyler Technologies SaaS starts ~$15,000/yr for small municipalities). A council can approve it without a procurement process.
- **Process reality:** local-government software decisions run on budget calendars — a few months to over a year when they miss a cycle. The pricing is designed so the decision does not need a grant, a bond, or a tax measure in most towns; the funding channels below are accelerants, not prerequisites.

## Q6. How can a town pay without new taxes?

1. **Federal safety grants.** NHTSA highway-safety funding flows through state highway safety offices: **$800M+ approved for FY2026** (Section 402: $409M; Section 405: $382M), with impaired-driving countermeasures an explicitly funded category ($147.5M in a recent cycle). Safe Streets (SS4A) is a second channel that funds local safety-action work directly.
2. **Offender cost recovery.** Many states authorize charging DUI offenders for emergency response. **Grass Valley, CA already charges $2.00 per minute of DUI investigation/response time** — at its ~37 DUIs/yr and the standard 2–6 officer hours, that is roughly **$13,300/yr recovered, covering the entire fee**.
3. **The Wisconsin model.** Road Crew was funded by a state DOT grant plus a per-drink surcharge at participating bars; SafeRide runs at roughly $1M/yr statewide funded through the Tavern League. Local hospitality co-funding is a proven third leg.

## Q7. Does a ride-home program actually reduce crashes?

Yes — this is the best-documented part of the model.

- **Road Crew (Wisconsin):** three rural communities, **19,757 rides in one year**, alcohol-related crashes down an estimated **17%** (~15 crashes prevented). Cost per avoided crash: **$15,300**, against an average alcohol-crash cost of ~$56,000 — a **~3.7× return** before counting injuries and deaths.
- **SafeRide (Tavern League of Wisconsin):** **45,527 free rides in 2022–23**, over **1 million rides since 2004**, ~$1M/yr program cost (~$11–$22 per ride). Wisconsin OWI convictions are down **44% since 2007**, with SafeRide credited as a contributor.
- **Break-even bar for a town:** at $12,000/yr and $15,300–$56,000 per avoided crash, a town's program pays for itself if it prevents **roughly one crash every 1–4 years** — or, on processing costs alone, ~2–5 DUIs a year.

## Q9. How do the drivers do?

- Drivers are **independent local operators**, not employees. They bill passengers directly and **keep 100% of the fare**; the platform never takes a cut. (Published fare estimates for comparable private rural rides run $15–$50 within ~10 miles, $75–$150 for longer runs.)
- The barrier that killed part-time driving is insurance: full commercial cover runs **~$1,200/month**. The network's model replaces it with **per-passenger-mile liability cover at $0.60/mile today** through HUM — pay only while carrying a passenger. Independent check: insurance runs ~21% of the average Uber/Lyft fare (Gridwise, 2026), ≈ $0.63/passenger-mile on a typical ride, so the per-mile rate is in line with market.
- At pilot-market scale, a town's rides represent an estimated **$15,000–$100,000/yr in local driver earnings** that currently leave town or never happen (modeled in the Brief, §16).

## Q10. How do you sign towns without burning the raise on sales?

The Version 2.0 research engine replaces the field-sales model:

1. **Automated screening** of all ~3,700 in-band towns on public data (taxi gone? rideshare absent? alcohol crashes rising?) — fractions of a dollar of compute per town plus review time, ~$1–$5 per town all-in (modeled).
2. **Public records requests** to each qualifying town's police, sheriff, and highway patrol for its actual DUI volume and cost. Under California's Public Records Act, fees are limited to duplication cost: **typically $0–$25 per request**, and the project's two pilot towns (Grass Valley and Nevada City) were researched at **$0 in fees**. Published schedules: Grass Valley $5 for the first 10 pages + $0.25/page; Nevada County Sheriff $5 first page + $0.50/page. Budgeted worst case per town: $50–$150 in fees, rare full-text searches excluded by template design.
3. **A pitch that arrives with the town's own numbers.** Total modeled cost per qualified town: **~$25–$100**. At a 2–5% close rate, **CAC ≈ $1,600–$2,950 per signed town**, paid back in **~2–5 months** of the fee.

## Q12. What are the honest risks?

- **Market smaller than early estimates.** The initial figure of 10,000–20,000 towns does not survive Census data; the verified band is ~3,700 and the serviceable market ~700. The plan is built on the corrected numbers ($8.4M ARR serviceable, before county contracts and price tiers).
- **Sales cycles are calendar-bound.** Small towns buy on annual budget cycles. The under-RFP-threshold price mitigates this; it does not eliminate it.
- **Regulation is state-by-state.** Transportation-for-hire and insurance rules differ by state, and the per-mile insurance model must be confirmed state by state before launch in each. This is budgeted in the legal/insurance line.
- **Records law has limits.** Agencies must produce existing records; they cannot be compelled to create new statistics. The research engine therefore requests source records and computes the figures itself — and in some jurisdictions, unusual requests can carry higher fees, controlled by a fee cap in every request template.
- **Insurance pricing is negotiated, not final.** The $0.60/mile rate is current; the ~$0.30 target depends on a future negotiation and scale. The unit economics above are run at the current rate.

## The data behind this pack

Datasets and sources assembled for this project (full index: `proofs/PROOFS.md`):

- **US Census Bureau** — incorporated-place counts and population distribution; USDA ERS non-metro data; OMB micropolitan delineations.
- **FBI Uniform Crime Reporting** — DUI arrest volumes (2021).
- **NHTSA / FARS** — alcohol-impaired fatalities, rural share, crash costs; NHTSA grant program releases (FY2026).
- **Two municipal DUI cost studies** — US Office of Justice Programs (California courts) and the Washington State Criminal Justice Cost Model (2023).
- **Two program evaluations** — University of Wisconsin / WisDOT (Road Crew) and Tavern League of Wisconsin (SafeRide) published results.
- **Municipal fee schedules** — City of Grass Valley FY2024-25; Nevada County Sheriff; California Public Records Act duplication-cost rules.
- **Vendor pricing** — Checkr (background checks, MVR, continuous monitoring); Gridwise rideshare economics (insurance share of fare); Tyler Technologies small-municipality SaaS benchmarks.
- **Pilot-market records** — Grass Valley (~12,000) and Nevada City (~6,000), CA: DUI context, agency fee schedules, and completed records research at $0 in fees.
- **The screening schema** — 18 fields captured per town (population, metro distance, taxi/rideshare status, alcohol-crash trend, DUI arrests, officer hours, taxpayer cost per DUI, records fees, qualification) — the dataset the Version 2.0 engine produces at national scale.
